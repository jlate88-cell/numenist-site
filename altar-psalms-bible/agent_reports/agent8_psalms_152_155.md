# THE SUPPRESSED PSALMS 152–155

## A Reference Document for the Practitioner

**Verified current date for this research: 2026-06-04 (Atlanta, EDT).**

---

## OVERVIEW

Four Davidic / Solomonic psalms exist in the **Syriac Peshitta tradition** (preserved as appendices to the 150-psalm Psalter in Syriac biblical manuscripts) and partially in the **Dead Sea Scrolls** — specifically the **Great Psalms Scroll, 11QPsa (11Q5)**, discovered in Cave 11 at Qumran in 1956 and first edited by **James A. Sanders** in 1965. Two of these psalms (154 and 155) survive in **Hebrew** from Qumran; two (152 and 153) survive only in **Syriac**.

The grouping was first identified in Western scholarship by the orientalist **Giuseppe Simone Assemani in 1759**. The standard scholarly reference is **James H. Charlesworth's *Old Testament Pseudepigrapha*, Volume 2 (Doubleday, 1985), pp. 609–624**, with **W. Wright's 1886** Syriac translation (Cambridge MS) as the foundational English edition. **Geza Vermes's *The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English* (Penguin, 7th ed.)** is the standard for the Qumran versions of 154 and 155.

## PSALM 152

**Syriac title:** *"Spoken by David when he was contending with the lion and the wolf which took a sheep from his flock."*

**Survives only in Syriac.** Likely composed in Hebrew, in the Land of Israel, during the **Hellenistic period (c. 323–31 BCE)**. Six verses. Non-rabbinic tone.

> 1. O God, O God, come to my aid; help me, save me, and deliver my soul from the slayer.
> 2. Will I go down to Sheol by the mouth of the lion? Will the wolf be the end of me?
> 3. Was it not enough for those who lay in wait for my father's flock, and tore a sheep of my father's flock — must they also wish the destruction of my own soul?
> 4. Have pity, O LORD, and save Your holy one from destruction, so that he may rehearse Your glories for all of his days, and may praise Your great name,
> 5. when You have delivered him from the hands of the destroying lion and of the ravening wolf, and when You have rescued my captivity from the hands of the wild beasts.
> 6. Quickly, my Lord, send from Yourself a deliverer, and draw me out of the gaping pit which imprisons me in its depths.

## PSALM 153

**Syriac title:** *"Spoken by David when returning thanks to God, who had delivered him from the lion and the wolf and he had slain both of them."*

**Survives only in Syriac.** Same Hellenistic-period horizon as 152. Six verses. Read as the companion piece — petition (152) and thanksgiving (153) as a paired diptych.

> 1. Praise the Lord, all you nations; glorify Him and bless His name;
> 2. For He delivered the soul of His Elect One from the hands of death; and He redeemed His Holy One from destruction.
> 3. And He saved me from the snares of Sheol; and brought me forth from the abyss that is inscrutable.
> 4. Because before my salvation could proceed from before Him, I almost became two parts by two beasts.
> 5. However, He sent His angel and closed from me the gaping mouths; and redeemed my life from destruction.
> 6. I myself shall praise Him and exalt Him because of all His graces, which He has provided and is providing for me.

## PSALM 154

**A wisdom psalm. Personifies Wisdom as a woman (cf. Proverbs 8, Sirach 1).** Twenty verses in the Syriac. **Verses 3–19 preserved in Hebrew in 11QPsa, column 18, lines 1–16** (also fragments in 4Q448). Composed in Hebrew, **before the 1st century CE**. A hymn of communal eating — fitting the Qumran *Yaḥad* context. Wright's older Syriac title problematically called it "The Prayer of Hezekiah," but Charlesworth and Sanders established the Davidic / wisdom horizon.

> 1. With a loud voice glorify God; proclaim His splendor in the congregation of the many.
> 2. Glorify His name in the multitude of the righteous, and celebrate His majesty with the faithful.
> 3. Bind your souls to the good and to the perfect, to glorify the Most High.
> 4. Assemble together to make His salvation known, and do not hesitate to make known His might and His majesty to all the simple.
> 5. For it is to make known the glory of YHWH that Wisdom has been given;
> 6. And it is for recounting His many deeds that she has been revealed to humans —
> 7. To make His power known to the simple, to explain His greatness to those lacking understanding,
> 8. Those who are far from her gates, those who have strayed from her entrances.
> 9. For the Most High is the Lord of Jacob, and His majesty is upon all His works.
> 10. A man who glorifies the Most High — He receives him as one who brings a meal offering,
> 11. As one who offers he-goats and bullocks, as one who fattens the altar with many burnt offerings, as a sweet-smelling fragrance from the hand of the righteous.
> 12. From the gates of the righteous her voice is heard, and from the assembly of the pious her song.
> 13. When they eat with satiety she is cited, and when they drink in community together.
> 14. Their meditation is on the Law of the Most High; their words to make known His power.
> 15. How far from the wicked is her word, from all the haughty to know her.
> 16. Behold, the eyes of YHWH have pity upon the good,
> 17. And upon those who glorify Him He increases His mercy; from an evil time will He deliver their soul.
> 18. \[Bless\] YHWH, who redeems the humble from the hand of strangers, \[and deliv\]ers the perfect from the hand of the wicked,
> 19. \[Estab\]lishing a horn out of Ja\[cob\], and a judge of \[peoples\] out of Israel.
> 20. He will spread His tent in Zion, and abide forever in Jerusalem.

**Hebrew incipit (11QPsa col. 18):** the surviving Hebrew opens at verse 3 with **בקול גדול פארו אלוהים** ("With a loud voice glorify God"). Sanders reconstructed verses 1–2 from the Syriac. The Syriac and Hebrew agree closely; the principal difference is that the Syriac preserves the opening couplet and the closing verses that are lost from the bottom of the Qumran column.

## PSALM 155

**A petitionary / penitential psalm. An incomplete acrostic** — the Hebrew at Qumran preserves bet (ב) through nun (נ); the Syriac extends the reconstruction to pe (פ). Sixteen verses. Compositional kin to Psalms 3, 22, 51, and 143. Preserved in 11QPsa (column 24) and in the Syriac Apocryphal Psalter.

> 1. O YHWH, I have called to You; be attentive to me.
> 2. I have spread forth my palms toward Your holy dwelling; incline Your ear and grant me my petition, and do not withhold my request from me.
> 3. Build up my soul and do not cast it down; and do not abandon it in the presence of the wicked.
> 4. May the Judge of Truth turn the rewards of evil away from me.
> 5. O YHWH, do not judge me according to my sins, for no living man is righteous in Your presence.
> 6. Give me discernment, O YHWH, in Your Law, and teach me Your ordinances,
> 7. So that many may hear of Your deeds, and peoples may honor Your glory.
> 8. Remember me, and do not forget me, and do not lead me into things too hard for me.
> 9. Cast the sin of my youth far from me, and may my transgressions not be remembered against me.
> 10. Purify me, O YHWH, from the evil scourge, and let it not turn again upon me.
> 11. Dry up its roots from me, and let its leaves not flourish within me.
> 12. You are my glory, O YHWH; therefore my request is fulfilled before You.
> 13. To whom shall I cry that he might grant to me, and the sons of men — what more can their power do?
> 14. My trust, O YHWH, is before You. I cried "YHWH!" and He answered me, and He healed my broken heart.
> 15. I slumbered and slept, I dreamed; indeed I awoke.
> 16. \[You sustained me, O YHWH\]; I shall call upon YHWH my Savior.

**Hebrew incipit (11QPsa col. 24):** **יהוה קראתיך הקשיבה אלי** (*YHWH qara'tikha, haqshivah elai* — "YHWH, I have called to you, attend to me"). The Syriac version contains a longer ending (the additional acrostic letters samekh, ayin, pe) absent from the Hebrew — likely either preserved from a fuller original or supplied by the Syriac translator to complete the acrostic. The Charlesworth/Sanders edition reads verses 12–15 with an explicit reference to **Belial** ("Do not let Belial dominate me, nor an unclean spirit; let neither pain nor the evil inclination take possession of my bones"), a phrasing characteristic of Qumran sectarian theology — strong evidence for the psalm's Second Temple Jewish origin and its place in the Qumran community's liturgy.

## HISTORICAL CONTEXT — WHEN AND WHY THESE WERE DROPPED

**Composition:** All four are pre-Christian Second Temple Jewish compositions, plausibly **3rd century BCE through 1st century BCE**. Psalms 154 and 155 are demonstrably Hebrew originals (Qumran proves it). Psalms 152 and 153 are most likely Hebrew or Aramaic originals later translated into Syriac, though no Hebrew original has been recovered.

**Preserved by:**
- The **Qumran community (Essenes)** kept 154 and 155 in their canonical Psalter — 11QPsa contains 150 + several additional compositions ordered differently from the Masoretic Text, suggesting the Qumran Psalter was a *different recension*, not an "appendix."
- The **Syriac Christian Church (Church of the East / Peshitta tradition)** preserved all five (151–155) appended to the standard 150 in Syriac biblical manuscripts. The crucial witness is the **Baghdad / Mosul manuscript, Library of the Chaldean Patriarchate 1113** (12th century, paleographically dated). They also appear in **Elias of al-Anbar's *Kethabha dhe-Dhurrasha* ("Book of Discipline")**, 10th century.

**Dropped from the Western canon because:**
1. The **proto-Masoretic Pharisaic / rabbinic tradition** that became normative after 70 CE (the Yavneh / Jamnia period) fixed the Hebrew Psalter at 150. The older theory that a formal "Council of Jamnia" decided the canon has been **largely discredited** since the 1960s; the closing of the Hebrew canon was a gradual process, and these psalms were simply not preserved in the proto-Masoretic stream.
2. The **Septuagint Greek tradition** preserved only Psalm 151 (as a supernumerary), not 152–155.
3. **Jerome's Latin Vulgate** followed the Hebrew (*Hebraica veritas*) and excluded everything beyond 150 + the Septuagint's 151.
4. **Protestant reformers** followed the Masoretic 150 and excluded even 151.
5. The **Syriac churches** alone preserved the full set — which is why these texts were unknown to Western Christendom until Assemani in 1759 and Wright in 1886, and unknown in Hebrew until Sanders published 11QPsa in 1965.

So the "suppression" is more accurately described as **non-preservation in the dominant Hebrew-Masoretic and Greek-Septuagint streams**, with continuous transmission only in the Syriac East and in the buried Qumran library that resurfaced in 1947–1956.
