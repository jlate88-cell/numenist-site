# The Latin Layer: Vulgate Psalter vs. Septuagint Psalter

## Lead: What Was Suppressed

The single most important fact about the Western Psalter is that **the Church did not adopt Jerome's translation from the Hebrew**. Jerome made one (the *Psalterium iuxta Hebraeos*, c. 392). It is technically the most accurate Latin Psalter ever produced. The Church refused to put it in the liturgy. Instead, the Catholic West chanted, prayed, memorized, and exorcised in the *Psalterium Gallicanum* — Jerome's earlier revision of a Greek translation of the Hebrew, made from Origen's Hexaplaric Septuagint around 386 CE. The Hebrew-direct Psalter survived in a handful of Spanish manuscripts and as a scholar's curiosity. The Greek-mediated Psalter became the bones of Western Christendom.

That choice — Greek over Hebrew, mediated text over source — is the editorial fork that determined which Psalms Coverdale translated, which Psalms entered the *Book of Common Prayer*, which Psalms the King James committee inherited, and which Psalms ended up on the candle-table of a working rootworker in 1940s Memphis. The cosmic frame: when a Catholic priest, an Orthodox monk, an Anglican vicar, and a Hoodoo doctor recite "Psalm 23," they are each holding a different downstream sediment of the same suppressed-source decision.

## 1. Jerome's Three Psalters

Jerome produced three Latin Psalters in roughly a decade. Modern scholarship has revised the traditional story but the working outline holds.

- **Psalterium Romanum (c. 384 CE).** Traditionally identified as Jerome's first revision of the Old Latin Psalter against the Septuagint, undertaken at Pope Damasus' request. More recent scholarship (cited in Wikipedia's *Latin Psalters*, drawing on De Bruyne and others) holds that what we call the "Roman" Psalter is actually one of several mid-4th-century Old Latin recensions that Jerome encountered rather than produced. He never claimed it. It became the liturgical text of Rome itself and survived in St Peter's Basilica into the 20th century.
- **Psalterium Gallicanum (c. 386–389 CE).** Jerome's revision of the Old Latin against **Origen's Hexapla** — the columnar comparison Origen had compiled at Caesarea, which gave Jerome access to a critically marked LXX with obeli (passages in Greek but not Hebrew) and asterisks (passages added from Theodotion to fill Hebrew material missing in the LXX). This is a Septuagint-based text, not a Hebrew translation. It got its name because it was promoted by Alcuin under Charlemagne and spread through Gaul from the 9th century onward. **This is the Psalter of the Vulgate.**
- **Psalterium iuxta Hebraeos (c. 392 CE).** Jerome's direct translation from the Hebrew. Stylistically the most precise of the three. It was never used liturgically. Spanish manuscripts preserved it longer than the rest of the West; Alcuin's Carolingian standardization wiped it elsewhere.

**Why did the Gallicanum win?** Three converging pressures:

1. **Liturgical inertia.** Monks and clergy had the LXX-flavored text memorized in chant. Replacing the Psalter is replacing the prayer-bones of the daily Office. Bogaert's manuscript work on the Vulgate transmission (summarized in the Vulgate Wikipedia entry, drawn from his *Revue Bénédictine* essays) shows that Alcuin's recension under Charlemagne (c. 800) deliberately retained the Gallicanum because it was already the *de facto* monastic text. By the time Trent ratified the Vulgate in 1546, the Gallicanum had been the liturgical Psalter for seven centuries.
2. **Apostolic argument.** The New Testament writers quoted the Septuagint. To prefer a fresh Hebrew translation over the LXX was to imply the apostles had been quoting an inferior text. Augustine made exactly this argument against Jerome (see §2). Müller's *First Bible of the Church* (1996) traces this position from Justin and Irenaeus forward: the LXX was held to be inspired translation, not mere translation.
3. **Anti-Jewish polemic disguised as method.** A Hebrew-direct Psalter implicitly conceded that contemporary Jewish text-tradition had something the Church lacked. Augustine's anxiety in Letter 71 is partly about congregational disturbance and partly about ceding interpretive ground to "the Jews" — he reports that the disrupted bishop in Oea had to send to local Jewish residents to adjudicate, and Augustine treats this as humiliating evidence against Jerome's method.

The decision served the institutional interest in continuity with the Greek-speaking apostolic past, the suppression of Jewish interpretive authority, and the protection of the Christological readings (see §3) that the LXX had already baked in.

## 2. Hebraica Veritas and Augustine's Letter 71

Jerome's principle was *Hebraica veritas* — Hebrew truth. Kamesar's argument (the *Quaestiones Hebraicae in Genesim* is the polemical scaffolding for the translation project) is that Jerome did not merely adopt a method; he had to construct a *theology* of textual priority that would justify going behind the LXX. He argued that the LXX translators worked under conditions of imperfect inspiration, that the apostles had quoted whichever text was at hand for rhetorical purposes, and that the *fons* — the source — outranked any translation.

Augustine, writing from Hippo in 403 CE (the letter is numbered 71 in Augustine's corpus, 104 in Jerome's), refused this:

> "I cannot sufficiently express my wonder that anything should at this date be found in the Hebrew manuscripts which escaped so many translators perfectly acquainted with the language."

He then narrates the **Jonah's gourd episode**. A bishop "of our brethren" had introduced Jerome's new translation of Jonah in his church at Oea (modern Tripoli). At Jonah 4:6 Jerome had rendered the Hebrew *qiqayon* as *hedera* (ivy) rather than the traditional Old Latin *cucurbita* (gourd) which followed the LXX's *kolokyntha*. Augustine reports:

> "Arose such a tumult in the congregation, especially among the Greeks, correcting what had been read, and denouncing the translation as false, that the bishop was compelled to ask the testimony of the Jewish residents (it was in the town of Oea). These... gave their testimony that it was correctly rendered in the Greek Septuagint version, and in the Latin one taken from it. What further need I say? The man was compelled to correct your version in that passage as if it had been falsely translated, as he desired not to be left without a congregation."

Augustine's argument is not philological. It is pastoral and political: a Hebrew-based Psalter and Old Testament will (a) split the Latin churches from the Greek churches whose Septuagint is the apostolic text, (b) leave bishops unable to defend their lectionary against challenges they cannot evaluate, and (c) implicitly elevate contemporary Jewish reading over apostolic Greek reading. His recommendation:

> "You would therefore confer upon us a much greater boon if you gave an exact Latin translation of the Greek Septuagint version."

Jerome refused. He answered in Letter 75 (Augustine's numbering) that the *qiqayon* is a shrub native to Palestine ("a kind of shrub having large leaves like a vine") that neither *cucurbita* nor *hedera* renders perfectly, and he chose *hedera* because the literal Syriac *ciceion* would mean nothing to a Latin congregation. He treats the Oea uproar as evidence of pastoral cowardice, not method failure.

Underneath the gourd is the structural anxiety: if the Hebrew can correct the Greek at one point, it can correct it anywhere — including at the Christologically loaded points the LXX had stabilized. Augustine sees this. Jerome sees this. Augustine chooses unity; Jerome chooses source. The Church chose Augustine. Williams' *Monk and the Book* reads Jerome's whole scholarly persona as the long-term winner anyway — the *image* of the scholar-monk in his study, Hebrew lexicon open, became the medieval and Reformation paradigm even as his actual Hebrew Psalter was ignored.

## 3. Concrete Textual Divergences

Three verses where the Gallicanum (= Vulgate liturgical Psalter) carries an LXX reading that the Hebrew Masoretic Text does not support, and that the Christian West has absorbed as scripture.

### Psalm 22:16 (Vulgate 21:17) — pierced vs. lion

| Tradition | Text | English |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrew (MT) | כָּאֲרִי יָדַי וְרַגְלָי (*ka'ari yaday w'raglay*) | "like a lion my hands and my feet" |
| Hebrew (5/6HevPs, Nahal Hever DSS) | כארו (*ka'aru*) | "they pierced/dug" |
| Greek (LXX 21:17) | ὤρυξαν χεῖράς μου καὶ πόδας (*ōryxan cheiras mou kai podas*) | "they dug my hands and feet" |
| Latin Vulgate (Gallicanum) | *foderunt manus meas et pedes meos* | "they pierced my hands and my feet" |

The Masoretic *ka'ari* ("like a lion") makes no grammatical sense — there is no verb. A verb is required. The Nahal Hever Psalms scroll (1st c. CE) reads *ka'aru*, the third-person plural perfect of *karah* ("to dig, bore through"). The LXX translators were reading a Hebrew text close to the Nahal Hever reading; Jerome inherited the LXX rendering through the Hexapla and passed it into the Gallicanum as *foderunt*. The crucifixion-prophecy reading of Psalm 22 stands or falls on this one consonant — *yod* vs. *waw*, two strokes — and the Latin West only ever read one side of it.

### Psalm 110:3 (Vulgate 109:3)

| Tradition | Text | English |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrew (MT) | מֵרֶחֶם מִשְׁחָר לְךָ טַל יַלְדֻתֶיךָ | "from the womb of the dawn, the dew of your youth is yours" |
| Greek (LXX 109:3) | ἐκ γαστρὸς πρὸ ἑωσφόρου ἐξεγέννησά σε | "from the womb before the morning-star I begot you" |
| Latin Vulgate | *ex utero ante luciferum genui te* | "from the womb before the day-star I begot you" |

The Hebrew is a difficult royal image: the king's young troops gather like dewdrops at first light. The LXX restructures the consonants and reads first-person divine generation: God speaks, "I begot you before the morning star." The Vulgate hardens this into *genui te* — the same verb the Nicene Creed uses for the eternal generation of the Son. The verse the West chants as a Christmas Vespers responsory is not what the Hebrew says. The Latin reading also carries the famous *in splendoribus sanctorum* — "in the splendors of the holy ones / saints" — for a Hebrew phrase that probably describes "holy garments" or "holy mountains." The trinitarian metaphysics rides on the Greek-Latin reading.

### Psalm 16:10 (Vulgate 15:10)

| Tradition | Text | English |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrew (MT) | לֹא־תַעֲזֹב נַפְשִׁי לִשְׁאוֹל לֹא־תִתֵּן חֲסִידְךָ לִרְאוֹת שָׁחַת | "you will not abandon my soul to Sheol, nor let your faithful one see the pit (*shachat*)" |
| Greek (LXX 15:10) | οὐδὲ δώσεις τὸν ὅσιόν σου ἰδεῖν διαφθοράν | "nor will you let your holy one see corruption (*diaphthora*)" |
| Latin Vulgate | *non dabis sanctum tuum videre corruptionem* | "you will not give your holy one to see corruption" |

The Hebrew *shachat* means "pit, grave" — a place. The LXX translators read the same consonants as *shichat* ("destruction, corruption") and gave *diaphthora*. Peter's Pentecost sermon (Acts 2:27) quotes the LXX exactly, and the entire apostolic argument for the resurrection — David's body decayed, therefore the psalm cannot refer to him, therefore it must refer to the Messiah whose body did not decay — is built on the LXX reading. The Hebrew says "pit"; the Greek and Latin say "corruption." Without the LXX reading, the apostolic proof-text dissolves.

This is what Augustine was protecting when he told Jerome to leave the LXX alone.

## 4. Psalm Numbering

The LXX/Vulgate and the Hebrew Masoretic Text agree on the total — 150 psalms — but draw the boundaries differently. The standard table:

| Masoretic (Hebrew) | Septuagint / Vulgate |
|---|---|
| 1–8 | 1–8 |
| **9–10** (two acrostic poems likely originally one) | **9** (kept together) |
| 11–113 | 10–112 |
| **114–115** | **113** (merged) |
| **116** | **114 + 115** (split) |
| 117–146 | 116–145 |
| **147** | **146 + 147** (split) |
| 148–150 | 148–150 |

Inheritance pattern:

- **Eastern Orthodox** and **Eastern Catholic** churches retain the Septuagint numbering. When an Orthodox monk says "Psalm 50," he means the *Miserere* (MT 51).
- **Roman Catholicism** used the Vulgate/Septuagint numbering in all official liturgical books until the post-Vatican II revisions; modern Catholic Bibles (NAB, Jerusalem Bible) use Hebrew numbering, often with Vulgate numbering in parentheses. The traditional Latin Mass and the Douay-Rheims still use Septuagint numbering.
- **Protestantism** inherited Hebrew numbering through the Reformation translators' deliberate return to the Masoretic text.
- **Anglicanism** is the interesting hybrid: it uses Hebrew numbering (Psalm 23 is 23) but a Latin-derived *text* (Coverdale, from the Vulgate). The number is Reformed; the verse is medieval.

## 5. Coverdale, Cranmer, and the Anglican Hybrid

Miles Coverdale's 1535 Psalter, the first complete printed English Bible, was not made from Hebrew. Coverdale could not read Hebrew. He worked from Luther's German, the Zürich Bible (Zwingli/Leo Jud), the **Latin Vulgate**, and Pagninus' Latin. Each psalm in Coverdale carries a Latin incipit drawn straight from the Vulgate. The text is materially Vulgate-flavored.

Coverdale revised his own Psalter for the Great Bible (1539–40), and that revision became the Psalter Cranmer placed in the 1549 *Book of Common Prayer*. When the King James Version appeared in 1611 with a fresh translation of the Psalms from the Hebrew, the Church of England declined to switch. The 1662 BCP kept Coverdale. The Episcopal Church kept Coverdale until 1979. Anglo-Catholic and traditionalist Anglican parishes still chant it.

Cranmer's reasoning was liturgical, not philological. The Coverdale Psalter sings. It is composed for the cadence of monastic chant, because it was made from a text composed for monastic chant. The KJV Psalter is more accurate to the Hebrew and harder to sing. Cranmer chose the singable text. Handel's *Messiah* — the apex of Anglican-Hebrew sacred music — uses the Coverdale BCP Psalter in several places, not the KJV. The liturgical-musical body of Anglicanism is Vulgate-derived flesh on a Reformed skeleton.

### The Anglican-Hoodoo pipeline

The transmission into African-American Hoodoo passes through Protestant — not Catholic — Bible culture in the American South. Catherine Yronwode's article "Secrets of the Psalms" (at LuckyMojo.com) and the *Jesterbear* psalms reference both document that the working text of Southern Hoodoo is **predominantly the King James Version**, not Coverdale, not Douay-Rheims. Two routes shape the practice:

1. **Direct KJV use**, especially for set-piece psalms (23 for blessing/protection, 35 for legal cases, 37 for patience, 91 for protection, 23+91 stacked for road-opening). The KJV Psalms are themselves a Hebrew-direct translation in numbering and at the word level, but they were made in a Church-of-England matrix that had been chanting Coverdale (Vulgate-flavored) for seven decades, so the rhythm and theology are Latinate even where the lexicon is Hebrew.
2. **Selig's *Secrets of the Psalms*** (Godfrey Selig, *Geheimniss der Psalmen*; English in the late 1930s, widely available through De Laurence and other occult publishers). This is a Lutheran-Pietist Christian-Kabbalistic manual that prescribes specific Psalms for specific operations — recited over oil, over water, over candles. It runs the Reuchlin-Christian-Kabbalah pipeline into African-American conjure practice. Hoodoo Psalm work in Memphis, New Orleans, and the Sea Islands shows clear Selig influence by the 1940s.

The Anglican retention of the Coverdale-Vulgate cadence shaped the *sound* of English-language Psalm devotion across the colonized Atlantic. Even where Hoodoo practitioners use the KJV text, the liturgical posture — psalm-as-spoken-charm, repeated, voiced, addressed to a personalized YHVH-as-Lord — is the inheritance of an Anglican prayerbook formed from a Latin Psalter whose source was Greek.

## 6. What the Practitioner Holds

The cascade, from source to working candle:

1. **Hebrew (MT, c. 100 CE crystallization).** A psalter that says *ka'ari* (lion), *shachat* (pit), *tal yaldutekha* (dew of your youth). Royal, embodied, ambiguous, irreducible. Anchored in the Temple and synagogue cycle.
2. **Septuagint (3rd c. BCE – 1st c. CE).** *Foderunt, diaphthora, ante luciferum genui te.* The translators were working from a Hebrew text-tradition that differed from the later Masoretic at decisive points (the Nahal Hever scrolls prove this is not Christian tampering — it is older Hebrew). Already Christologically loaded by the apostolic period; already the Bible the apostles quote. **Loss at this step:** the consonantal ambiguity of the Hebrew, the bodily concreteness, the Temple-cycle situatedness. **Gain:** a Bible that already speaks in the metaphysical register Hellenistic Judaism and early Christianity will inherit.
3. **Vulgate Gallicanum (386 CE).** Jerome's revision of the LXX through Origen's Hexapla, displacing his own better Hebraicum. *Foderunt* stays. *Corruptionem* stays. *Genui te* stays. **Loss:** Jerome's own Hebrew-direct readings, suppressed for liturgical continuity. **Gain:** a singable Latin text that becomes the spine of the Divine Office for 1100 years.
4. **Douay-Rheims (OT 1609–10).** The first major English Catholic Bible, translated from the Vulgate. Reproduces the Vulgate Psalter into Latinate English. *"They have dug my hands and my feet... thou wilt not give thy holy one to see corruption."* **Loss:** Coverdale's musicality, since Douay is a more wooden Latin-to-English equivalence. **Gain:** the Vulgate readings carried verbatim into vernacular Catholic devotional life.
5. **English Catholic devotional use (17th c. onward).** Psalm-novenas, the *Officium Parvum BVM*, the Penitential Psalms in vernacular and Latin, the burial Psalms. The Christological readings are now ambient — a Catholic reading Psalm 22 *sees crucifixion* in *foderunt*, sees Christ in *genui te*, sees the resurrection in *corruptionem*. The Hebrew alternatives are invisible.
6. **Hoodoo (19th–20th c.).** Selig's *Secrets of the Psalms* and the KJV provide the operational text. The Psalms are deployed as charms: spoken, written on paper, placed under candles, sealed in jars, dressed with oil, called over water. The metaphysical preconditions for treating a Psalm as a working charm — the Psalm as an active force that does something when voiced — were established by the Vulgate-Anglican liturgical tradition that taught the West to chant Psalms as performative speech to the divine, not as didactic literature. **Loss at this step:** explicit awareness that the text being deployed has been through four languages and at least three theological filters. **Gain:** the Psalm as operative instrument, lineage-charged through the entire transmission chain.

The practitioner using Psalm 23 or Psalm 91 in active working should know: the version in your hand is not "the Psalm." It is a sedimentary deposit. The Hebrew lion, the Hebrew pit, the Hebrew dew were edited out at the Greek step. The Greek piercing, the Greek corruption, the Greek begetting were preserved by the Latin step against Jerome's own better Hebrew translation. The Latin singability was preserved by the Anglican step against Reformation pressure for accuracy. Every step was a choice. Every choice served an interest. The working still works — it works *through* the sedimentary chain, not despite it — but the operator should know what is buried under each verse.
