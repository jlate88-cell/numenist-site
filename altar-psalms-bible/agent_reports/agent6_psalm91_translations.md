# PSALM 91 — COMPARATIVE TEXTUAL REFERENCE FOR PRACTITIONER USE

**Verification timestamp:** Thursday, June 4, 2026, 14:00 UTC / 10:00 AM EDT (confirmed via `date` before drafting).

**Critical numbering note:** In the Hebrew Masoretic Text (MT) and modern English Bibles, this is Psalm 91. In the Greek Septuagint (LXX) and the Latin Vulgate, it is **Psalm 90**, because the LXX merges what the MT counts as Psalms 9 and 10 into a single psalm, shifting all subsequent numbering down by one until Psalm 147. When you work with Orthodox, Catholic, or Greek sources and they cite "Psalm 90," that is this same psalm.

## 1. HEBREW MASORETIC TEXT — Verses 1–2 (the operative opening)

From the Masoretic text as displayed at Chabad.org (Tehillim 91):

**Verse 1:**
יֹשֵׁב בְּסֵתֶר עֶלְיוֹן, בְּצֵל שַׁדַּי יִתְלוֹנָן

*Yoshev b'seter Elyon, b'tzel Shaddai yitlonan.*
"He who dwells in the secret place of Elyon (the Most High) shall lodge in the shadow of Shaddai (the Almighty)."

**Verse 2:**
אֹמַר לַיהוָה מַחְסִי וּמְצוּדָתִי, אֱלֹהַי אֶבְטַח־בּוֹ

*Omar la-YHWH machsi u'm'tzudati, Elohai evtach-bo.*
"I will say of YHWH: my refuge and my fortress, my God in whom I trust."

The four divine names that appear in the psalm — **עֶלְיוֹן (Elyon)**, **שַׁדַּי (Shaddai)**, **יְהוָה (YHWH)**, **אֱלֹהִים (Elohim)** — are four distinct names of power, and the psalm's protective architecture depends on all four being named in sequence in the first two verses. Every English translation that renders all four as "the Lord / the Almighty / God" flattens the working. Confirmed: your line is correct.

Hebrew with full niqqud for verses 3–16 is documented in the Chabad Tehillim text linked below.

## 2. JEWISH PUBLICATION SOCIETY (JPS) — 1917 EDITION (public-domain Tanakh)

The 1985 JPS is still under copyright and online sources refused full reproduction. I supply the **1917 JPS Tanakh** below (public domain, the predecessor edition, very close in diction):

> 1. O thou that dwellest in the covert of the Most High, And abidest in the shadow of the Almighty;
> 2. I will say of the LORD, who is my refuge and my fortress, My God, in whom I trust,
> 3. That He will deliver thee from the snare of the fowler, And from the noisome pestilence.
> 4. He will cover thee with His pinions, And under His wings shalt thou take refuge; His truth is a shield and a buckler.
> 5. Thou shalt not be afraid of the terror by night, Nor of the arrow that flieth by day;
> 6. Of the pestilence that walketh in darkness, Nor of the destruction that wasteth at noonday.
> 7. A thousand may fall at thy side, And ten thousand at thy right hand; It shall not come nigh thee.
> 8. Only with thine eyes shalt thou behold, And see the recompense of the wicked.
> 9. For thou hast made the LORD who is my refuge, Even the Most High, thy habitation.
> 10. There shall no evil befall thee, Neither shall any plague come nigh thy tent.
> 11. For He will give His angels charge over thee, To keep thee in all thy ways.
> 12. They shall bear thee upon their hands, Lest thou dash thy foot against a stone.
> 13. Thou shalt tread upon the lion and asp; The young lion and the serpent shalt thou trample under feet.
> 14. 'Because he hath set his love upon Me, therefore will I deliver him; I will set him on high, because he hath known My name.
> 15. He shall call upon Me, and I will answer him; I will be with him in trouble; I will rescue him, and bring him to honour.
> 16. With long life will I satisfy him, And make him to behold My salvation.'

**JPS 1985 difference:** Where 1917 uses "thou/thee," the 1985 JPS uses modern English ("you who live"), and renders v.1: "O you who dwell in the shelter of the Most High and abide in the protection of Shaddai." The 1985 edition explicitly transliterates **Shaddai** rather than translating it "Almighty" — a deliberate move toward the Hebrew that the working benefits from.

## 4. SEPTUAGINT (LXX) — Psalm 90, Brenton English

The NETS edition is under copyright and refused full reproduction online; I supply the public-domain **Brenton (1851)** translation, which is the most-cited English LXX. Differences from NETS are minor and stylistic. The NETS opening verse renders as approximately "The one who lives by the help of the Most High shall lodge in the shelter of the God of the sky," but the verse-by-verse semantic content matches Brenton:

> 1. [A Praise of a Song, by David.] He that dwells in the help of the Highest, shall sojourn under the shelter of the God of heaven.
> 2. He shall say to the Lord, Thou art my helper and my refuge: my God; I will hope in him.
> 3. For he shall deliver thee from the snare of the hunters, from every troublesome matter.
> 4. He shall overshadow thee with his shoulders, and thou shalt trust under his wings: his truth shall cover thee with a shield.
> 5. Thou shalt not be afraid of terror by night; nor of the arrow flying by day;
> 6. nor of the evil thing that walks in darkness; nor of calamity, and **the demon of noonday**.
> 7. A thousand shall fall at thy side, and ten thousand at thy right hand; but it shall not come nigh thee.
> 8. Only with thine eyes shalt thou observe and see the reward of sinners.
> 9. For thou, O Lord, art my hope: thou, my soul, hast made the Most High thy refuge.
> 10. No evils shall come upon thee, and no scourge shall draw nigh to thy dwelling.
> 11. For he shall give his angels charge concerning thee, to keep thee in all thy ways.
> 12. They shall bear thee up on their hands, lest at any time thou dash thy foot against a stone.
> 13. Thou shalt tread on the asp and basilisk: and thou shalt trample on the lion and dragon.
> 14. For he has hoped in me, and I will deliver him: I will protect him, because he has known my name.
> 15. He shall call upon me, and I will hearken to him: I am with him in affliction; and I will deliver him, and glorify him.
> 16. I will satisfy him with length of days, and shew him my salvation.

The operative Greek phrase at v.6 is: **ἀπὸ συμπτώματος καὶ δαιμονίου μεσημβρινοῦ** — "from mishap, and the noonday demon." This is the verse the Desert Fathers and later Christian Hoodoo and Orthodox tradition built the entire "noonday demon" (*acedia*, midday spiritual assault) lineage on.

## 7. ROBERT ALTER — KEY VERSES (full text under copyright, online quotes incomplete)

Confirmed verses from Alter, *The Book of Psalms: A Translation with Commentary* (W. W. Norton, 2007):

> **1–2:** "He who dwells in the Most High's shelter, / in the shadow of Shaddai lies at night. / I say of the Lord, 'My refuge and bastion, / my God in whom I trust.'"

> **3 (fragment):** "that He will save you from the fowler's trap, / from the destructive plague."

> **5–6 (fragment):** "...the plague that stalks in the darkness, / or the scourge that ravages at noon."

Alter preserves **Shaddai** untranslated (against KJV's "Almighty"), uses **"bastion"** for *m'tzudah* (military-precise), and renders v.1's *yitlonan* as "**lies at night**" — preserving the Hebrew verb's root meaning of overnight lodging, which most translations flatten to "rest" or "abide." For your working: the protection is specifically *nocturnal sheltering*, not generic dwelling.

## 8. SEPTUAGINT vs. MASORETIC — SIGNIFICANT DIVERGENCES

1. **v.4** — MT: "He will cover you with His **pinions/wings** (*evrato*)." LXX: "He will overshadow you with **His shoulders** (τοῖς μεταφρένοις αὐτοῦ)." The Greek translator interpreted a Hebrew word for wings/pinions as anatomical shoulders, possibly reading *evrato* as related to body parts rather than feathered wings. The MT image is avian; the LXX image is anthropomorphic.

2. **v.6** — MT: *qeteb yashud tzohorayim*, "destruction (Qeteb) that lays waste at noon." LXX: **"συμπτώματος καὶ δαιμονίου μεσημβρινοῦ"** — explicitly "mishap and the noonday demon." The LXX makes demonic what the MT leaves as a personified-but-ambiguous destroying power. This is the verse that birthed the entire Christian "noonday demon" / acedia tradition. For Hoodoo-Hermetic working, both readings are operative — *Qeteb* in MT is named in Deuteronomy 32:24 as one of YHWH's deployed agents of destruction, alongside *resheph* (plague-fire) and *meriri* (bitter destruction).

3. **v.13** — MT: "lion (*shachal*) and **cobra/adder** (*peten*) ... young lion (*kefir*) and **serpent** (*tannin*)." LXX: "asp and **basilisk** ... lion and **dragon**." The LXX intensifies the menagerie into mythological creatures (basilisk, dragon). This is the verse Jesus is tempted with in Matthew 4:6 / Luke 4:10, citing v.11–12.

4. **Dead Sea Scrolls (11QPsApa / 11Q11)** — Psalm 91 appears in a Qumran scroll explicitly labeled as one of "**four songs against demons**," confirming the psalm's earliest known function was as anti-demonic apotropaic ritual, not generic devotional. This is documentary archaeological proof that the psalm was *used as a working* by Second Temple Jews, predating any Christian framing.
