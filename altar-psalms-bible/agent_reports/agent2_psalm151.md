## I. Psalm 151 — Greek Septuagint (LXX), Brenton's English Translation (1844, public domain)

**Superscription:** *"This Psalm is a genuine one of David, though supernumerary, composed when he fought in single combat with Goliad."*

1. I was small among my brethren, and youngest in my father's house: I tended my father's sheep.
2. My hands formed a musical instrument, and my fingers tuned a psaltery.
3. And who shall tell my Lord? the Lord himself, he himself hears.
4. He sent forth his angel, and took me from my father's sheep, and he anointed me with the oil of his anointing.
5. My brothers were handsome and tall; but the Lord did not take pleasure in them.
6. I went forth to meet the Philistine; and he cursed me by his idols.
7. But I drew his own sword, and beheaded him, and removed reproach from the children of Israel.

(Brenton, *The Septuagint with Apocrypha*, London, 1851.) The more modern NETS translation by Albert Pietersma (Oxford 2007) is under copyright and not reproduced here; it is the academic standard if you want the contemporary scholarly rendering.

## II. Psalm 151A — Dead Sea Scrolls, 11QPsa (Column XXVIII)

The Hebrew text was first published by James A. Sanders in 1963 in *Discoveries in the Judaean Desert* IV, *The Psalms Scroll of Qumrân Cave 11 (11QPsa)*. The Sanders translation and Vermes' rendering in *The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English* are both under copyright. The substance, paraphrased:

**Superscription:** *"A Hallelujah of David, son of Jesse."* (Hebrew: *Halleluyah le-David ben-Yishai* — הללויה לדויד בן ישי)

The Hebrew Psalm 151A opens with David declaring he was smaller than his brothers, the youngest son of his father, and that he was made shepherd over his father's flock and ruler over his father's young goats. He fashioned a pipe with his hands and a lyre with his fingers, and so rendered glory to YHWH — David says within himself that the mountains do not witness to Him, nor do the hills proclaim Him; the trees of His words, and the flocks of His deeds, do. The psalm asks: *who can declare and who can speak and who can recount the deeds of the Lord?* — God has seen all, heard all, attended to all. He sent His prophet to anoint him; Samuel came to elevate him. His brothers went out to meet Samuel — fine of form, fine of appearance: the tall of stature, the handsome of hair — yet the Lord God did not choose them. Rather, He sent and took David from behind the flock, anointed him with holy oil, and set him as prince (*nagid*) to His people and ruler among the sons of His covenant.

The 11QPsa Hebrew Psalm 151A is roughly twice the length of LXX verses 1-5 and contains material the Greek translator excised — most notably the "mountains/hills/trees/flocks" theological meditation between the shepherding and the anointing.

## III. Psalm 151B — Dead Sea Scrolls, 11QPsa

Psalm 151B in the scroll is fragmentary; the bottom of column XXVIII is damaged and only the opening lines survive. The superscription reads as something like *"At the beginning of David's power, after the prophet of God had anointed him,"* and the surviving lines record David seeing the Philistine taunting from the enemy lines — the text breaks off before the combat itself. The narrative of Goliath's defeat (LXX verses 6-7) is implied to follow but is lost in the lacuna.

The DSS therefore preserve **two psalms** (anointing + Goliath) where the LXX preserves **one combined seven-verse psalm**.

## IV. Comparison — LXX vs. DSS

| | **LXX Psalm 151** | **11QPsa Psalms 151A + 151B** |
|---|---|---|
| Language | Greek (translated from a lost Hebrew *Vorlage*) | Hebrew (the original tradition) |
| Form | One psalm, 7 verses | Two distinct psalms |
| 151A content | Compressed: shepherd → instruments → anointing → brothers passed over | Expanded: includes the "mountains do not witness, but trees and flocks do" theological meditation entirely absent from the Greek |
| Order | Brothers' rejection placed *after* anointing (v.5) | Brothers' rejection placed *during* the anointing narrative |
| Goliath section | Two compact verses (vv.6-7) | Separate psalm (151B), fragmentary |
| Superscription | Lengthy descriptive title | Brief: "Hallelujah of David son of Jesse" |

The scholarly consensus (Sanders, Skehan, Haran, Smith) is that the LXX translator worked from a Hebrew source close to but not identical with 11QPsa, then **condensed and reorganized** the material — flattening the two-psalm structure into one, and removing the meditative middle section that did not narratively serve the David-vs.-Goliath frame. Sanders famously described 151A as "a poetic midrash on 1 Samuel 16:1-13."

## V. Hebrew Opening (Superscription)

**הללויה לדויד בן ישי** — *Halleluyah le-David ben-Yishai* — "A Hallelujah of David, son of Jesse."

This is the only Davidic psalm in 11QPsa that opens with *Halleluyah* rather than *le-David* alone — a deliberate liturgical framing, marking the psalm as praise rather than petition.

## VI. Historical / Lineage Context — Why It Was Dropped

**The psalm was canonical in the Greek-speaking East from the start.** Codex Sinaiticus (4th c.), Codex Alexandrinus (5th c.), and the Old Greek Psalter all include it, usually marked *exōthen tou arithmou* — "outside the number," meaning a 151st in addition to the 150-psalm Hebrew canon.

**The suppression in the West traces to Jerome (c. 347-420).** When Jerome translated the Latin Vulgate, he made the deliberate methodological choice to follow the *Hebraica veritas* — the Hebrew text — rather than the Greek Septuagint. Because Psalm 151 was not in the Hebrew Masoretic tradition Jerome had access to, he classed it (along with several other Greek-only books) as **apocryphal**: useful for edification, not canonical for doctrine. His correspondence with Augustine on this point is preserved; Augustine resisted, but Jerome's text won institutional dominance.

**The Council of Trent (1546)** formalized the Roman Catholic Old Testament canon at 46 books and explicitly *excluded* Psalm 151 along with 1-2 Esdras and the Prayer of Manasseh — even though it had been printed in Vulgate editions as an appendix. The Protestant Reformers, following Luther's Hebrew-only principle, dropped it more aggressively still.

**The Greek and Russian Orthodox** churches retained it as canonical and still read it liturgically — particularly during preparation for the sacrament of Holy Unction.
**The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church** preserved it as canonical within their 81-book Bible, the broadest Christian canon in continuous use.
**The Syriac Peshitta** tradition includes it, alongside Psalms 152-155 (the latter four found only in Syriac manuscripts and partially confirmed by 11QPsa).
**The Armenian and Coptic** Bibles also preserve it.

**The Dead Sea Scrolls (1956 / unrolled 1961-62)** vindicated the Eastern tradition: the discovery of a *Hebrew* Psalm 151A and 151B at Qumran demolished the long-standing Western claim that the psalm was a late Greek pseudepigraphal composition with no Hebrew original. It is, in fact, a genuine Second Temple Hebrew composition that the Masoretic editors chose to exclude when they fixed the 150-psalm canon — likely between the 1st and 2nd centuries CE, possibly in conscious differentiation from sectarian (Qumranic, early Christian) collections that included it.

**Summary of the suppression chain:** Masoretes excluded the Hebrew → Jerome followed the Masoretes against the Greek → Trent confirmed Jerome → Protestants reinforced Trent's exclusion → Western Christianity inherited a 150-psalm Psalter, while the East (Greek, Russian, Ethiopian, Syriac, Coptic, Armenian) kept the 151st intact.
